In the projects I work on, cybersecurity often comes up through practical questions from customers: how do we protect the systems our business depends on, where should we start, and how can we focus on the risks that matter most? Those questions are increasingly important for organisations of every size. Digital systems support critical business activities, store valuable information, and connect organisations with their customers and partners. As our dependence on these systems grows, so does the potential impact of a security incident.

When cybersecurity comes up in a conversation, however, the discussion often moves quickly towards technical solutions: firewalls, identity and access management (IAM), virtual private networks (VPNs), endpoint protection, and other security technologies. These are important tools, but focusing on them too early risks missing a more fundamental question: **what are we actually trying to protect, and why does it matter to the business?**

## Cybersecurity Is a Business Responsibility

Cybersecurity is often perceived as the responsibility of the IT department, or even a dedicated security team in larger organisations. While these teams have an essential role in protecting systems and implementing appropriate controls, the responsibility for managing business risk cannot be delegated to technology specialists alone.

Consider a manufacturing company that depends on a digital production planning system. If that system becomes unavailable following a cyberattack, the consequences extend far beyond IT. Production may stop, deliveries may be delayed, customers may lose confidence, and the organisation may face financial losses. The IT department can work to restore the system, but the business must determine which operations are most critical, what level of disruption is acceptable, and which investments are justified to reduce the risk.

The same principle applies to a small business, even when it has no dedicated IT department or security specialist. A compromised email account, an unavailable invoicing system, or the loss of customer information can have a disproportionate impact on a smaller organisation. The scale may differ, but the underlying responsibility remains the same: understand the risks and make informed decisions about how to address them.

## Start with Risk, Not Technology

A pragmatic approach to cybersecurity begins by understanding what could go wrong and what the consequences would be. Which business activities are essential? Which systems and information support them? What would happen if those systems were compromised, unavailable, or accessed by unauthorised parties?

These questions help shift the discussion from technology to business impact. They also provide a more useful basis for deciding where to invest time and money. Not every system requires the same level of protection, and not every conceivable threat deserves the same priority. The objective is to make deliberate choices based on the organisation's activities, its exposure to risk, and the consequences it would face if an incident occurred.

There is a useful parallel with insurance. Organisations assess potential losses and decide which risks they can accept, which they should reduce, and which they may be able to transfer financially. Cybersecurity follows a similar logic, although insurance alone cannot prevent an incident or eliminate all its consequences. Understanding risk is what allows an organisation to make sensible decisions about prevention, preparedness, and recovery.

## Technology Is Part of the Solution

Starting with the business does not make technology any less important. Firewalls help control network traffic, IAM helps manage access to systems and information, and VPNs can provide secure connections between users and organisational resources. These and other security measures can be essential components of a well-designed solution.

The important distinction is that technology should follow from an understanding of the problem, rather than become the starting point by default. Implementing a security tool without understanding the risk it is intended to address may create a false sense of security, leave important gaps, or consume resources that would be better spent elsewhere.

Effective cybersecurity also involves people, processes, responsibilities, and the way systems are designed and connected. Employees need to understand their responsibilities, access rights must reflect actual needs, and organisations need to know how they will respond when something goes wrong. These aspects cannot be addressed through technology alone; they require coordinated decisions across the business and IT.

## The Enterprise Architect as a Coordinator

This is where the Enterprise Architect can make a valuable contribution. The architect connects business strategy and objectives with the capabilities, processes, information, applications, and technology needed to achieve them. Cybersecurity belongs within that broader picture, rather than being treated as a separate technical concern.

The architect's role is not to replace security specialists or take ownership of every security decision. Instead, it is to help business stakeholders, security experts, and technology teams work towards a coherent solution. This means ensuring that risks are understood in their business context, that security requirements influence architectural decisions, and that the proposed solutions address the needs of the organisation as a whole.

This role is just as relevant in a small or medium-sized business as it is in a large enterprise. The responsibilities may be shared between fewer people, and formal architecture practices may be less developed, but the need to connect business priorities with technology decisions remains.

## A Business-First Approach to Cybersecurity

Pragmatic cybersecurity is not about choosing between business needs and technical protection. It is about bringing them together, understanding the risks that matter, and making informed decisions about how to address them.

The starting point is therefore not a firewall, a security product, or a checklist of technical controls. It is an understanding of the business, what it depends on, and what it stands to lose. From there, business leaders, architects, and technical specialists can work together to build an approach to cybersecurity that is proportionate, coherent, and fit for purpose.
